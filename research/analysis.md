# Headache Analysis

## 2026-03-22 — Initial Assessment

### Background
- 29-year-old male, 188 cm, 85 kg
- Lifelong headache history, not recent onset
- No history of neck injury
- Desk/computer worker
- No physiotherapist or cervical spine evaluation to date
- Had headaches before and after Arthur was born (not parenthood-related)
- Scheduling PT at Accelerate Physical Therapy, Newport Beach (in-network Aetna PPO, $0/visit)

### Ergonomics and Lifestyle Factors
- **Morning desk setup**: external monitor at proper height — good
- **On the go**: works on laptop in various settings for several hours most days — sustained cervical flexion with no ergonomic support, likely a significant daily contributor
- **Sleep position**: stomach sleeper with pillow — forces sustained end-range cervical rotation (90°) for hours overnight. Strong candidate for why headaches are sometimes already at threshold on waking.
- **Stretch routine**: daily passive ROM (head turns up/down/sideways, shoulder rolls, lateral tilts, chin forward/back). Missing: deep cervical flexor activation (chin tucks), suboccipital release, and any strengthening component. The routine maintains mobility but doesn't address the likely weakness/motor control deficits.
- **Caffeine**: 3-4 cups coffee/day, first thing in the morning through ~15:00-16:00. Moderate, consistent intake — unlikely a trigger unless routine is disrupted (weekend schedule change, travel).
- **Vision**: no glasses or contacts — not a factor
- **No neck injury history** — cervical dysfunction is functional, not post-traumatic

### Demographic Considerations
- Male sex makes classic migraine less likely as primary driver (3:1 F:M ratio) and strengthens the cervicogenic hypothesis given the presentation
- Cluster headache (male-predominant, peaks at this age) doesn't fit: episodes are too long, lack autonomic symptoms (eye tearing, nasal congestion), and aren't excruciatingly severe
- At 29 with no history of neck injury, cervical dysfunction is almost certainly muscular/functional (weakness, tightness, motor control) rather than degenerative — the most treatable kind

### Data Reviewed
76 free-text diary entries (Jan 5 – Mar 21, 2026) covering both headache and headache-free days. Entries include sleep scores, activity, neck state, weather, medication, and context.

Roughly 18-20 headache days out of ~75 days tracked (~25% of days).

### Working Hypotheses

**Primary: Cervicogenic component is dominant**
Neck stiffness is mentioned in nearly every entry — headache and non-headache days alike. Critically, multiple entries describe pain explicitly *radiating from the neck*:
- "smärtan kom just från axlarna" (Feb 15)
- "strålar från baksidan av nacken i mitten" (Jan 17)
- "kändes som att det skulle komma från nacken och sprida sig över huvudet" (Mar 15)
- "strålar från övre delen av nacken" (Mar 21)

Pain locations cluster at the occipital region, temples (especially left), and top of head — all consistent with referred pain from upper cervical structures (C1-C3).

The daily stretch routine (passive ROM only) hasn't resolved the stiffness — confirmed to be missing deep cervical flexor work and any strengthening. Stomach sleeping likely loads the upper cervical spine overnight, and laptop use in non-ergonomic settings adds sustained cervical flexion during the day.

**Secondary: Heat exposure as a unifying trigger (exercise, sauna, weather)**
Multiple trigger contexts share a common mechanism — elevated core/head temperature:
- Running in heat (Jan 18, Mar 15), golf in sun (Jan 12), walking in 28°C (Jan 14, Jan 17, Feb 1)
- Sauna (Mar 2, Mar 6), jacuzzi (Jan 18)
- Hot weather on sedentary days (Feb 9)

Critically, hydration does NOT prevent these headaches. Walter reports that drinking large amounts of water (and electrolytes like Gatorade) before, during, and after both running and sauna does not change the outcome. This rules out simple dehydration and points to heat itself as the mechanism.

Exercise-triggered headaches follow a specific pattern: waking with a subclinical "light, light" headache → running (even slow zone 2 for 5km) escalates it into a full-day headache. The headache feels "released" by the run rather than caused by it. This suggests the cervical system is already primed (possibly from overnight neck position/loading), and exercise tips it past the threshold via:
- Repetitive impact transmitting force to the cervical spine
- Sustained deep neck muscle activation for head stabilization
- Core temperature elevation lowering the headache threshold

Sauna follows a similar pattern — heat causes vasodilation and muscle relaxation, followed by rebound tightening of already-chronically-tight upper cervical muscles. Head position in the sauna (unsupported, often forward or extended) adds cervical loading. The mechanism overlaps with alcohol (also a vasodilator), explaining why sauna + alcohol together (Mar 6) is particularly provocative.

**Tertiary: Alcohol**
Clear association (Feb 15 wine, Mar 6 party). Vasodilation likely lowers the headache threshold in someone with an already-sensitized cervical/trigeminal system.

### Patterns That Don't Correlate Cleanly
- **Sleep**: Headaches occur with sleep scores of 99 and 67 alike. No clear linear relationship, though accumulated sleep debt may matter (Mar 18 after 6h39min night).
- **Stress/workload**: Some headache-free days were extremely long and stressful (Feb 11-13). The Jan 28 panel talk is the only clear stress-triggered near-miss.
- **Movement**: Sedentary days sometimes trigger headaches (Feb 4, Feb 9), but many sedentary days are fine. Regular walking seems mildly protective but not sufficient.

### What Ibuprofen Tells Us
400mg ibuprofen reliably resolves episodes, suggesting an inflammatory or muscular mechanism rather than pure vascular (migraine). This is consistent with the cervicogenic hypothesis.

### Overall Picture
Most likely a primary headache disorder (migraine or tension-type) with a strong cervicogenic component. The neck appears to be both a constant low-grade issue and the main escalation pathway to full headache. The common thread across triggers (exercise, sauna, hot weather, alcohol) appears to be **heat/vasodilation acting on a sensitized cervical-trigeminal system**, not dehydration. The cervical spine remains the common denominator — it's the substrate on which all these triggers act.
