## 1. Global Burden

Headache disorders are among the most prevalent neurological conditions worldwide.

- **~2.9 billion** individuals affected globally (GBD Study 2023, *Lancet Neurology* 2025)
- Age-standardised prevalence: **34.6%** of the global population
- Cause **541.9 years lived with disability (YLDs) per 100,000** population
- **Migraine alone drives ~90% of the burden**
- Females have a YLD rate more than **twice** that of males (739.9 vs 346.1 per 100,000)
- Over **20% of headache-attributed YLDs** stem from medication-overuse headache (MOH), a preventable complication

**Key insight for personal analysis:** The female-male disparity and the significant role of medication overuse suggest hormonal factors and treatment patterns are critical variables to track.

## 2. Classification (ICHD-3)

The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3, published 2018 in *Cephalalgia*) is the universal diagnostic standard. Translated into 20+ languages.

### 2.1 Primary Headaches (the disorder *is* the headache)

#### Migraine
- **Without aura:** Recurrent attacks lasting 4–72 hours. Unilateral, pulsating, moderate-to-severe intensity. Aggravated by routine physical activity. Associated with nausea and/or photophobia + phonophobia.
- **With aura:** Preceded by fully reversible visual, sensory, or speech/language symptoms (5–60 min). Visual aura most common (zigzag lines, scotoma).
- **Chronic migraine:** ≥15 headache days/month for >3 months, of which ≥8 have migraine features.
- **Subtypes:** Hemiplegic, basilar, retinal, vestibular migraine.
- Prevalence: ~12–15% of adults. 3:1 female-to-male ratio.

#### Tension-Type Headache (TTH)
- Most common primary headache (~38% lifetime prevalence).
- **Episodic:** Bilateral, pressing/tightening (non-pulsating), mild-to-moderate. **Not** aggravated by physical activity. No nausea. At most one of photophobia or phonophobia.
- **Chronic TTH:** ≥15 days/month for >3 months.
- Often co-occurs with migraine, making diagnosis challenging.

#### Cluster Headache (CH)
- Severe, strictly unilateral orbital/supraorbital/temporal pain lasting 15–180 min.
- Accompanied by ipsilateral autonomic symptoms: lacrimation, nasal congestion, ptosis, miosis, eyelid oedema, facial sweating.
- Attacks occur in clusters (bouts) of weeks to months with remission periods.
- Prevalence: ~0.1%. 3–4:1 male-to-female ratio (opposite of migraine).

#### Other Primary Headaches
- Other trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias (paroxysmal hemicrania, SUNCT/SUNA, hemicrania continua)
- Primary cough headache, primary exercise headache, primary stabbing headache
- New daily persistent headache (NDPH)

### 2.2 Secondary Headaches
Caused by another underlying condition:
- Head/neck trauma
- Vascular disorders (e.g., subarachnoid haemorrhage)
- Non-vascular intracranial disorders (e.g., raised ICP)
- Substance use or withdrawal (including medication-overuse headache)
- Infection, disorders of homeostasis

### 2.3 Painful Cranial Neuropathies and Facial Pain
- Trigeminal neuralgia, occipital neuralgia, etc.

**Key insight for personal analysis:** Determining *which type(s)* of headache you experience is the foundation. Features to track: location (unilateral vs bilateral), quality (pulsating vs pressing), severity (1–10), duration, associated symptoms (nausea, photo/phonophobia, autonomic signs), and effect of physical activity.

## 3. Pathophysiology

### 3.1 Migraine

Migraine is now understood as a **complex neurovascular disorder**, not simply a vascular phenomenon.

#### The Trigeminovascular System (TVS)
- Sensory fibers from the trigeminal nerve (V1 division) innervate meningeal blood vessels.
- Activation leads to release of vasoactive neuropeptides, primarily **CGRP** (calcitonin gene-related peptide), plus **substance P** and **PACAP** (pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide).
- CGRP causes vasodilation, neurogenic inflammation, and pain signal transmission to the trigeminal nucleus caudalis (TNC) in the brainstem.
- From TNC, signals ascend to thalamus → cortex (pain perception).

*(Goadsby et al., 2017, Physiol Rev; Edvinsson et al., 2019, J Headache Pain)*

#### Cortical Spreading Depression (CSD) / Spreading Depolarization
- A slowly propagating wave (~3 mm/min) of neuronal and glial depolarization across the cortex, followed by sustained suppression.
- **Underlies migraine aura**: visual aura corresponds to the wave crossing visual cortex.
- CSD also appears to **trigger headache** by releasing noxious chemicals that accumulate in tissues and CSF, reaching levels sufficient to activate pial trigeminal afferents ("Buildup Hypothesis").
- 2024 milestone: First direct evidence of CSD captured via intracranial EEG in a human during somatosensory aura (published in *PNAS*, 2024).
- **Astrocytes** play a dual role: initially protective (clearing K+ and glutamate), but later harmful when energy demand exceeds compensatory capacity, releasing lactate and exacerbating hypoxia.

*(Moskowitz, 2025, Cephalalgia; Frontiers in Mol Neurosci, 2024)*

#### Serotonin (5-HT)
- Central serotonergic dysfunction contributes to migraine. Low serotonin states are associated with migraine susceptibility.
- Triptans work by activating 5-HT₁B/₁D receptors → vasoconstriction + inhibition of CGRP release + central inhibition of pain.

#### Central Sensitization
- Repeated activation of trigeminovascular pathways leads to sensitization of second-order neurons in the TNC.
- Manifests clinically as **cutaneous allodynia** (scalp tenderness, discomfort from glasses/earrings).
- A shift from **activity-dependent** to **activity-independent** central sensitization may explain progression from episodic to chronic migraine.

#### Genetics
- Large-scale GWAS (102,000+ migraine cases, 771,000+ controls) identified **123 susceptibility loci** (86 previously unknown).
- Implicated genes: CALCA/CALCB (CGRP), HTR1F (serotonin 1F receptor), CACNA1A (calcium channel), HMOX2, MPPED2, FECH.

### 3.2 Tension-Type Headache

- Both **peripheral** (nociception from pericranial myofascial tissue) and **central** mechanisms involved.
- Episodic TTH: mainly peripheral excitability (muscle tenderness, myofascial trigger points).
- Chronic TTH: clearly involves **central sensitization** (similar to chronic migraine but distinct pathway).
- **CGRP appears to play a lesser role** than in migraine — CGRP-targeted therapies are not proven effective for TTH.
- The trigeminovascular system is involved in both, but the activation/processing of nociceptive input differs.

*(J Headache Pain, 2023, "Debate: differences and similarities between TTH and migraine")*

### 3.3 Cluster Headache

- Three principal neuroanatomical components: (1) trigeminovascular system, (2) trigeminal-autonomic reflex (sphenopalatine ganglion), (3) hypothalamic system.
- **Posterior hypothalamus activation** demonstrated via PET during attacks.
- Strong circadian/circannual periodicity linked to hypothalamic clock function.
- CGRP is also elevated during cluster attacks.

*(Coppola et al., 2024, Cephalalgia; Frontiers in Pain Research, 2024)*

**Key insight for personal analysis:** The biology suggests that tracking CGRP-related symptoms (throbbing, sensitivity) and signs of central sensitization (allodynia, spreading pain) could help distinguish headache types and severity patterns.

## 4. Triggers and Risk Factors

The top 10 most frequently reported migraine triggers in the literature:
**Stress, fatigue/sleep issues, fasting, auditory stimuli, visual stimuli, olfactory stimuli, hormonal changes, sleep changes, weather, and alcohol.**

### 4.1 Environmental Triggers

| Trigger | Evidence |
|---------|----------|
| **Weather / barometric pressure** | ~20% of migraine episodes associated with weather changes. Higher humidity, rainfall, and barometric pressure changes correlated with headache in a 2023 smartphone-app study (4,375 adults). AI-driven analysis of 330,000+ headache episodes found barometric pressure, humidity, and rainfall can predict migraine occurrence. |
| **Light (photophobia)** | Bright or flickering light is a well-established trigger. Blue-green wavelengths (~480 nm) most provocative. Melanopsin-containing retinal ganglion cells implicated. |
| **Noise** | Auditory triggers among top 10. Loud or sustained noise can provoke attacks, likely via thalamic sensitization. |
| **Odors** | Osmophobia (aversion to smells) affects ~25–50% of migraineurs. Perfume, cigarette smoke, chemical fumes are common triggers. |
| **Altitude** | Acute high altitude (>2500m) can trigger headache via hypoxia and changes in cerebral blood flow. |

### 4.2 Dietary Triggers

| Trigger | Evidence |
|---------|----------|
| **Alcohol** | Especially red wine. Histamine, tyramine, and sulfites implicated. Even small amounts can trigger in susceptible individuals. |
| **Caffeine** | Dual role: trigger in 6.3–14.5% of migraineurs, but also acute treatment adjuvant. **Caffeine withdrawal** is a well-established trigger (onset 12–24h after last intake). Chronic overuse (>200 mg/day) increases chronification risk. |
| **Fasting / skipping meals** | Among the most robust triggers. Hypoglycemia and metabolic changes implicated. |
| **Dehydration** | Insufficient fluid intake is an independent trigger. |
| **Specific foods** | Chocolate, aged cheese, processed meats (nitrates/nitrites), MSG, aspartame, citrus fruits. Evidence varies — many may be **prodromal cravings** misidentified as triggers (especially chocolate). |

### 4.3 Sleep

- Both **too little** and **too much** sleep can trigger headaches.
- **Sleep schedule irregularity** (e.g., sleeping in on weekends) is particularly provocative.
- Strong bidirectional relationship: headache disrupts sleep, poor sleep triggers headache.
- Migraineurs more often have early chronotypes. 40.2% report early-morning attack onset.
- Shift work significantly increases migraine risk — proposed as "Shift Work Migraine Disorder" (2024).

### 4.4 Stress and Psychological Factors

- **Stress is the #1 reported trigger** across studies.
- The "let-down" effect: headaches often occur when stress *decreases* (weekends, holidays), not during peak stress itself.
- Comorbidity with anxiety (OR ~2–5x) and depression (OR ~2–4x).
- Stress is thought to act via HPA axis activation, cortisol fluctuations, and changes in serotonin availability.

### 4.5 Hormonal Factors

- Menstrual migraine: attacks tied to estrogen withdrawal in the late luteal / early menstrual phase.
- Oral contraceptives can worsen or improve migraine depending on the individual.
- Pregnancy often provides relief (especially 2nd/3rd trimester), but postpartum period is high-risk.
- Perimenopause: often worsening; post-menopause: often improvement.

### 4.6 Physical Activity and Posture

- Vigorous exercise can trigger attacks acutely in some (~10–20%), but regular moderate exercise is **preventive**.
- Sustained poor posture (forward head position) contributes to TTH via pericranial muscle tension.
- Neck pain is both a trigger and a prodromal symptom of migraine.

### 4.7 Screen Time and Eye Strain

- Prolonged screen use contributes via: blue light exposure, sustained accommodation, reduced blink rate, fixed posture.
- Not well-studied as an isolated trigger but part of the "modern lifestyle" trigger complex.

### 4.8 Medication Overuse

- Using acute medications too frequently leads to **medication-overuse headache (MOH)**.
- Thresholds: triptans/opioids/combination analgesics ≥10 days/month; simple analgesics/NSAIDs ≥15 days/month.
- The AMPP study showed progression risk increases at even lower frequencies: 5 days/month for butalbital, 8 days for opioids, 10 days for triptans.
- **MOH accounts for >20% of all headache-attributed disability globally** (GBD 2023).
- Treatment: withdrawal of overused medication for 8–12 weeks + preventive therapy.

**Key insight for personal analysis:** Track all of these as variables in your data: sleep timing & duration, stress level, weather/barometric pressure, dietary intake (caffeine, alcohol, meals), menstrual cycle if applicable, exercise, screen time, and medication use with exact frequency.

## 5. Patterns, Prodrome, and Chronification

### 5.1 Circadian Patterns

- **Migraine:** Clear circadian pattern in ~50% of patients. Trough between 23:00–07:00, peak onset **06:00–12:00** (morning predominance). This is consistent with hypothalamic/circadian clock involvement.
- **Cluster headache:** Even stronger circadian pattern. Attacks often at fixed times, frequently 1–2 hours after sleep onset (first REM period).
- **Genetic basis:** 110 of 168 migraine susceptibility genes are **clock-controlled genes**. Core circadian genes CK1δ and RORα associated with migraine.

*(Neurology, 2023; J Headache Pain, 2021)*

### 5.2 Seasonal Patterns

- Cluster headache: strong seasonal periodicity (spring and autumn most common).
- Migraine: less clear due to geographic/methodological variation. However, studies from Norway reported consistent yearly peak timing. Some evidence for increased migraine in spring (April–October period showing broad circannual peak).

### 5.3 Prodrome (Premonitory Phase)

Occurs **2–48 hours before headache onset** in up to ~77% of migraineurs. The hypothalamus is the likely driver.

**Common prodromal symptoms:**
- Yawning (most specific)
- Mood changes (irritability, euphoria, depression)
- Fatigue / low energy
- Neck stiffness/pain
- Food cravings (especially sweet/chocolate — easily confused with dietary "triggers")
- Increased urination (polyuria)
- Difficulty concentrating
- Sensitivity to light/sound (can begin in prodrome)

**Neurochemistry:** Hypothalamic release of orexin, neuropeptide Y, and dopamine regulates sleep–wake states, appetite, and sensation — all of which shift during the prodrome.

*(PMC, 2024, "The prodrome of migraine: mechanistic insights and emerging therapeutic strategies")*

### 5.4 Aura Phase

- Present in ~25–30% of migraineurs.
- Typically 5–60 minutes before or at headache onset.
- Visual most common (scintillating scotoma, fortification spectra).
- Can also be sensory (tingling, numbness) or speech/language disturbance.
- Caused by cortical spreading depression (see Pathophysiology).

### 5.5 Chronification: Episodic → Chronic

**Risk factors for chronification:**
- High attack frequency (>4 per month)
- Medication overuse
- Obesity (BMI >30)
- Caffeine overuse
- Snoring / sleep apnea
- Depression and anxiety
- Stressful life events
- Head/neck injury
- Female sex
- Low socioeconomic status

**Mechanism:** Repeated trigeminovascular activation → progressive central sensitization → lowered threshold → more frequent attacks → vicious cycle.

**Key insight for personal analysis:** Tracking prodromal symptoms alongside headache episodes could reveal a personal "early warning system" with 24–48h lead time. Also, monitoring attack frequency over time is critical for detecting any trend toward chronification.

## 6. Data-Driven Approaches to Headache Management

### 6.1 Headache Diaries

- The **standard tool** in headache medicine for decades.
- Effective for: establishing diagnosis, identifying triggers, monitoring treatment response, detecting medication overuse.
- Digital diaries (smartphone apps) have improved compliance and data quality.
- Recommended minimum variables to track: date/time, duration, intensity (0–10), location, quality, associated symptoms, potential triggers, medications used, menstrual status.

### 6.2 Most Predictive Variables (from ML studies)

Studies using machine learning on diary + wearable data have identified these as **most predictive** of next-day attacks:

| Variable | Source |
|----------|--------|
| **Pain intensity of prior attacks** | Stubberud et al., 2023, Cephalalgia |
| **Menstrual cycle phase** | Stubberud et al., 2023 |
| **Medication effectiveness** | Stubberud et al., 2023 |
| **Pain location** | Stubberud et al., 2023 |
| **Barometric pressure changes** | Deep learning on 330,000+ episodes |
| **Humidity and rainfall** | Deep learning on 330,000+ episodes |
| **Electrodermal activity** | Wearable sensor study (XGBoost, 81% accuracy) |
| **Skin temperature** | Wearable sensor study |
| **Sleep patterns** | Multiple studies |

### 6.3 Machine Learning Approaches

| Study | Method | Performance |
|-------|--------|-------------|
| Stubberud et al. (2023, *Cephalalgia*) | KNN on mobile phone diary + wearable data | **91% recall, AUC 0.83** for next-day prediction |
| Wearable biosignal study (2024) | XGBoost on pre-migraine biomarker patterns | **81% accuracy** |
| Deep learning on smartphone diary (2024) | Deep learning on 330,000+ episodes | Significant accuracy for weather-driven prediction |
| General AI survey (Stubberud et al., 2024, *Cephalalgia*) | Various | Accuracies ranging 40–83% |

*(Stubberud et al., 2024, "Artificial intelligence and headache", Cephalalgia)*

### 6.4 Personalized Trigger Identification

- **Population-level triggers ≠ individual triggers.** This is a critical insight.
- N-of-1 study designs (single-patient experiments) are considered the gold standard for trigger identification.
- Challenge: many triggers operate in **combination** rather than isolation (e.g., sleep deprivation + stress + weather change).
- Machine learning can detect non-linear multi-factor interactions that simple diary review cannot.
- Recommended approach: structured diary for ≥3 months → exploratory analysis → hypothesis testing of suspected triggers.

**Key insight for personal analysis:** Your data collection is the right approach. Aim for ≥3 months of consistent tracking. Include both headache days and headache-free days (for contrast). Track multiple variables per day. Consider weather data (can be merged from historical APIs). ML approaches like random forests or gradient boosting can help identify your personal trigger profile.

## 7. Prevention and Treatment

### 7.1 Acute (Abortive) Treatment

| Treatment | Evidence Level | Notes |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| **NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin) | Level A | First-line for mild-moderate attacks. Risk of MOH at ≥15 days/month. |
| **Triptans** (sumatriptan, rizatriptan, etc.) | Level A | First-line for moderate-severe migraine. Work via 5-HT₁B/₁D. Risk of MOH at ≥10 days/month. |
| **Gepants** (ubrogepant, rimegepant) | Level A | CGRP receptor antagonists. Oral. No vasoconstrictive risk (safe in cardiovascular disease). No MOH risk observed so far. |
| **Ditans** (lasmiditan) | Level A | 5-HT₁F agonist. No vasoconstrictive effect. Causes dizziness/sedation. |
| **Combination analgesics** | Level A | Paracetamol + aspirin + caffeine. Effective but higher MOH risk. |
| **Oxygen (15 L/min)** | Level A (for CH) | Standard acute treatment for cluster headache. |

### 7.2 Preventive (Prophylactic) Treatment

#### Pharmacological

| Treatment | Evidence | Notes |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| **CGRP monoclonal antibodies** | Level A — now **first-line** (AHS 2024) | Erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab (monthly SC), eptinezumab (IV quarterly). Response rates: 45.9% at 3 months → 71.0% at 24 months. Well-tolerated. |
| **Beta-blockers** (propranolol, metoprolol) | Level A | Traditional first-line. Contraindicated in asthma. |
| **Anticonvulsants** (topiramate, valproate) | Level A | Topiramate: weight loss common side effect. Valproate: teratogenic. |
| **Antidepressants** (amitriptyline, venlafaxine) | Level B | Especially useful with comorbid depression/anxiety/TTH. |
| **OnabotulinumtoxinA** (Botox) | Level A (chronic migraine only) | 155–195 units across 31–39 injection sites every 12 weeks. Also effective in MOH. |
| **Gepants** (rimegepant, atogepant) | Level A | Dual use: both acute and preventive. |
| **Verapamil** | Level A (for CH) | First-line preventive for cluster headache. |

*(AHS consensus 2024; Lancet, 2025 — "CGRP-targeted therapy in migraine")*

#### Nutraceuticals / Supplements

| Supplement | Dose | Evidence | Mechanism |
|------------|------|----------|-----------|
| **Magnesium** (citrate/oxide) | 400–600 mg/day | Level B | ATP synthesis, vascular tone regulation, NMDA receptor antagonism |
| **Riboflavin** (B2) | 400 mg/day | Level B | Mitochondrial energy metabolism (Complex I/II support) |
| **CoQ10** | 100–300 mg/day | Level C | Mitochondrial electron transport chain, antioxidant |
| **Feverfew** | 50–300 mg/day | Level B | Anti-inflammatory, inhibits platelet aggregation |
| **Melatonin** | 3–5 mg at night | Level C | Circadian rhythm regulation; may be as effective as amitriptyline in one RCT |
| **Butterbur** (Petasites) | 75 mg BID | Level A (but safety concerns) | Anti-inflammatory. Removed from some guidelines due to hepatotoxicity risk. |

A combination of **riboflavin 400 mg + magnesium 600 mg + CoQ10 150 mg** showed reduction in maximal pain intensity and headache impact in a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind multicenter trial (Gaul et al., 2015, *J Headache Pain*).

*(Nutraceuticals and Headache 2024, Current Pain and Headache Reports, 2025)*

### 7.3 Non-Pharmacological Approaches

| Approach | Evidence | Notes |
|----------|----------|-------|
| **CBT (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy)** | Level A | Reduces attack frequency. Addresses stress, maladaptive coping, pain catastrophizing. As effective as some preventive medications. |
| **Relaxation training** | Level A | Progressive muscle relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing. Can reduce attack frequency. |
| **Biofeedback** | Level A | EMG and thermal biofeedback. Meta-analysis shows significant reduction in frequency and severity vs. waiting list. Synergistic when combined with pharmacotherapy. Home-based biofeedback now validated. |
| **Mindfulness-based therapies** | Level B | MBSR and MBCT show promise for reducing attack frequency and disability. |
| **Regular aerobic exercise** | Level A | 30–40 min, 3–5x/week. Moderate intensity (jogging, cycling, swimming). As effective as topiramate in one RCT. Mechanism: endorphins, serotonin modulation, stress reduction. |
| **Acupuncture** | Level A | Cochrane review: superior to sham and equal to prophylactic drugs. Particularly for TTH. |
| **Sleep hygiene** | High clinical importance | Consistent bedtime/wake time, adequate duration (7–8h), dark/quiet room. |

*(Behavioral interventions for migraine prevention, systematic review & meta-analysis, 2025)*

### 7.4 Neuromodulation

| Device/Method | Target | Evidence | Notes |
|---------------|--------|----------|-------|
| **sTMS (single-pulse TMS)** | Visual cortex | Level B | FDA-cleared. Acute + preventive. Interrupts CSD. |
| **nVNS (non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation)** — gammaCore | Cervical vagus nerve | Level B–C | FDA-cleared. Acute migraine + cluster. 1.48 more headache-day reduction vs. placebo. Mixed primary endpoint results. |
| **Cefaly (external trigeminal nerve stimulation)** | Supraorbital branch of V1 | Level B | FDA-cleared for prevention. 20 min/day. |
| **REN (remote electrical neuromodulation)** — Nerivio | Upper arm conditioned pain modulation | Level B | FDA-cleared for acute + preventive. Smartphone-controlled. |
| **Sphenopalatine ganglion stimulation** | SPG | Level B (for CH) | Implanted device. Efficacy in cluster headache. |
| **Deep brain stimulation** | Posterior hypothalamus | Experimental | For refractory cluster headache. ~42% significant pain reduction in open-label studies. |

*(Update on Neuromodulation, Current Pain and Headache Reports, 2024; IHS guidelines, Cephalalgia, 2025)*

## Appendix: Recommended Variables for Personal Headache Data Analysis

Based on the literature, the following variables are most valuable for identifying personal patterns:

### Per-Episode Variables
- Date and time of onset
- Duration (hours)
- Intensity (0–10 NRS)
- Location (unilateral L/R, bilateral, frontal, temporal, occipital)
- Quality (pulsating, pressing, stabbing, other)
- Associated symptoms (nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, aura, allodynia, autonomic)
- Medication taken (type, dose, time, effectiveness 0–10)

### Daily Context Variables (track every day, not just headache days)
- Sleep: bedtime, wake time, perceived quality (1–5), interruptions
- Stress level (1–10)
- Mood (1–10 or categorical)
- Meals: timing, skipped meals, notable foods (alcohol, caffeine amount, cheese, chocolate, processed meat)
- Caffeine: type and quantity (mg)
- Hydration: approximate water intake
- Physical activity: type, duration, intensity
- Screen time: total hours
- Menstrual cycle day (if applicable)
- Weather: temperature, humidity, barometric pressure (can be sourced from historical weather APIs)
- Medications: all taken that day (including supplements, preventives)
- Notable events: travel, altitude change, strong odors, illness

### Derived Variables (for analysis)
- Day of week / weekend vs weekday
- Hours of sleep (calculated)
- Sleep regularity (deviation from mean bedtime)
- Caffeine withdrawal (hours since last intake)
- Barometric pressure delta (change from previous day)
- Cumulative stress (rolling average)
- Attack frequency (rolling 30-day count — for chronification monitoring)
- Medication use frequency (rolling 30-day count — for MOH monitoring)
