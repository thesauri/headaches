# Neurology Referral Research — 2026-08-13

Context: after 4 months of PT with real but incomplete improvement, the physiotherapist recommended widening the search to neurology. His working diagnosis: **cervicogenic headache + cervical instability (provoked by running or golf)**. A re-run VBI (vertebrobasilar insufficiency) provocation test came back **partially positive: dizziness and double vision (diplopia)** on sustained head positioning.

This note synthesizes the full diary dataset (Jan 5 – Aug 13, 189 entries), the initial research doc, the PT's clinical findings, and new literature research into: (1) candidate underlying causes, ranked; (2) what kind of neurologist to see; (3) the workup to expect and ask about.

## 1. First, calibrate the VBI test result

Before building on the VBI finding, it needs a reliability caveat. The positional VBI screen (sustained rotation/extension) is a contested test:

- Systematic reviews find it has **poor sensitivity and questionable validity** — ultrasound studies show it doesn't consistently reduce vertebral artery flow even in healthy people, and false positives are common ([Physiopedia](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Vertebral_Artery_Test); [Hutting et al., Musculoskeletal Science & Practice 2020](https://www.mskscienceandpractice.com/article/S2468-7812(20)30118-1/fulltext); [ScienceDirect debate](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2468781219301274)).
- Dizziness on sustained rotation has benign alternative explanations: **cervicogenic dizziness** (noisy proprioceptive input from the same suboccipital region already implicated — note the gaze-stabilization drill was prescribed for exactly this) and benign positional vestibular responses.
- **However: diplopia is not one of those benign explanations.** Double vision is a brainstem/posterior-circulation-territory sign, not a proprioceptive one. A reproducible positional diplopia deserves imaging regardless of the screening test's poor statistics.

So: the test result alone proves nothing, but the *diplopia component* is the one piece that genuinely raises the vascular hypothesis and justifies vascular imaging. It should be neither dramatized nor dismissed.

**Interim precautions (until imaged/cleared):**
- No cervical manipulation (thrust adjustments) — this was already relevant given the Jun 2 atlas "knax."
- Avoid sustained end-range neck rotation/extension (deep over-shoulder checks, prolonged overhead extension, extreme sauna head positions).
- Know the spontaneous red flags — the "5 D's": sudden **d**izziness, **d**iplopia, **d**ysarthria (slurred speech), **d**ysphagia, **d**rop attacks, plus facial/limb numbness or ataxia occurring *without* provocation → that's urgent/emergency care, not a scheduled appointment.

## 2. Candidate underlying causes, ranked

### Hypothesis A — Migraine with prominent cervical prodrome (probability: high)

The strongest *unifying* explanation for the full 7-month dataset, and the one the cervicogenic framing may have been obscuring. The trigger list accumulated in the diary is a nearly complete textbook migraine trigger list ([Migraine Trust](https://migrainetrust.org/live-with-migraine/self-management/common-triggers/); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8088284/)):

| Diary finding | Classic migraine trigger? |
|---|---|
| Alcohol (Feb 15, Mar 6, May 27/29, Aug 5) | Yes — one of the most common |
| Let-down / "klassisk helghuvudvärk" (Apr 13) | Yes — textbook let-down effect |
| Sleep deprivation (May 25, Mar 18) **and oversleeping (Jul 16: headache after best sleep in a week)** | Yes — *change* in sleep, both directions, is the trigger |
| Missed/late meals (Jul 16: lunch at 14:00; Jul 27: "åt alltså lite för lite") | Yes — fasting is among the most-reported triggers |
| Exertion (running, hiking, golf) | Yes — recognized trigger |
| Heat/weather | Yes — recognized trigger |
| Stress crunch (Apr 8–10 RAVE) | Yes — most common trigger of all |
| Jun 14: "rejäl migrän," cold sweats, couldn't eat | Migraine phenotype with autonomic/GI features |

Key supporting facts:
- **Neck pain/stiffness occurs in the majority of migraine attacks as a prodrome** — muscle stiffness "especially in the neck and shoulders" is listed prodrome symptomatology ([American Migraine Foundation](https://americanmigrainefoundation.org/resource-library/timeline-migraine-attack/)). The trigemino-cervical convergence means migraine *produces* neck symptoms; the arrow can point brain→neck, not only neck→brain ([PMC: neck pain in differential diagnosis of migraine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9955923/)).
- The **stiffness–headache decoupling** (Jul 14 note: headaches vanished while stiffness stayed at 2) is awkward for a purely cervicogenic model but unremarkable for migraine-with-neck-prodrome.
- The **incomplete PT response** with the PT's own comment ("usually sees more improvement, faster") fits a mixed picture where PT fixed the cervicogenic *contribution* but the primary generator is central.
- The morning prodrome Walter can now reliably feel and abort with preventive ibuprofen (4/4 successes) behaves like a migraine premonitory phase.
- Misdiagnosis in both directions is well documented — CGH ticks migraine boxes and vice versa ([StatPearls CGH](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK507862/)).

Against it: male sex (though ~6% of men have migraine — hardly disqualifying), no aura reported, unilaterality not fixed, and pain repeatedly *described* as radiating from the neck. None of these exclude migraine.

**Why this matters:** migraine has a deep, specific, modern treatment arsenal (triptans/gepants acutely; CGRP-class preventives) that has never been tried. A single triptan trial on the next full-blown attack is a cheap, high-information diagnostic probe to propose to the neurologist.

### Hypothesis B — Cervicogenic headache ± cervical instability (the PT's hypothesis; probability: moderate, likely as a *component*)

The evidence for a real cervical contribution is too strong to discard: reproducible L≫R findings across three independent channels (two PTs' palpation, the band-exercise block, lateralized morning stiffness), pain explicitly radiating from the occiput/upper neck, the golf/rotation trigger, and genuine PT-attributable gains (the two clean stretches, reduced clustering and severity).

The **instability** claim specifically is the newest and least verified part. Relevant facts:
- The Jul 27–30 cluster proved **golf triggers without heat** — mechanical rotation is sufficient. This is the single best datapoint for the instability/mechanical hypothesis (and simultaneously consistent with exertion-triggered migraine — the hypotheses overlap here).
- Refractory-CGH literature: predictors of refractoriness include limited cervical ROM and multisource pain; escalation options beyond PT exist — **diagnostic medial branch blocks / greater occipital nerve (GON) block, radiofrequency ablation** ([Frontiers in Neurology 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2025.1531180/full); [Practical Neurology 2025](https://practicalneurology.com/archives/may-jun-2025-issue/cervical-spine-considerations-in-headache-management/35799/)).
- A **GON or C2–C3 block is the closest thing to a diagnostic gold standard** for cervicogenic headache — if anesthetizing the cervical source abolishes the headache, that's strong causal evidence; if it doesn't, weight shifts decisively to migraine. This is a specific thing to request.
- True instability is assessed with **dynamic imaging**: flexion-extension radiographs, rotational CT for C1–C2 (loss of facet overlap at maximal rotation), or upright/dynamic MRI ([PMC: post-traumatic atlanto-axial instability](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9964642/); [PMC: craniocervical reference values](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10020271/)). Static supine MRI can miss it. Note: no trauma history and no known hypermobility syndrome, which makes significant structural instability less likely a priori — an exam for generalized hypermobility (Beighton score) is a quick box to check.

### Hypothesis C — Rotational vertebral artery compression (Bow Hunter's syndrome) or other posterior-circulation pathology (probability: low, but the one that must be ruled out)

This is what the positive VBI test + rotation-triggered symptoms would look like if real: **dynamic, reversible occlusion/compression of a vertebral artery during head rotation**, producing vertigo/dizziness, visual disturbance (including diplopia), presyncope — relieved on returning to neutral ([StatPearls: Rotational Vertebral Artery Syndrome](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559022/); [JNS Case Lessons](https://thejns.org/caselessons/view/journals/j-neurosurg-case-lessons/1/9/article-CASE20169.xml)).

- It is **rare**, usually involves a dominant vertebral artery with a hypoplastic/compromised contralateral side, and is most often caused by osteophytes or fibrous bands — degenerative features unusual at 29.
- But it is *diagnosable* (MRA/CTA of the neck; if suspicion persists, dynamic/rotational angiography is the definitive test) and *treatable*, and the cost of missing true positional vertebral artery compression is posterior-circulation TIA/stroke.
- Also in this family, worth one look: **vertebral artery dissection** (unlikely — symptoms would be more acute and persistent) and congenital variants (VA hypoplasia is common and benign alone, but changes the math when combined with rotational compression).

### Hypothesis D — Chiari I malformation / posterior fossa lesion (probability: low, cheap to exclude)

Exertion-triggered suboccipital headache is the classic Chiari I presentation, and about half of true cough/exertional-headache consults have a structural lesion, ~90% of those Chiari I ([González-Quintanilla et al., Cephalalgia 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03331024221146989)). Against it: Walter's exertional headaches build over hours rather than the classic brief Valsalva-triggered pattern, and there are no other Chiari signs. A single brain MRI (which the workup will include anyway) settles it — tonsillar descent ≥5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal imaging.

### Ruled out / stayed unlikely
- **Cluster headache** — excluded on phenomenology since March.
- **Medication-overuse headache** — currently ~8 ibuprofen days/month vs threshold ≥15, but the trend is up and preventive use is growing. Worth stating the count to the neurologist proactively.
- **Simple dehydration, screen time, vision** — repeatedly shown non-correlated in the structured data.

## 3. What kind of neurologist

**Primary recommendation: a fellowship-trained / UCNS-certified headache medicine specialist**, ideally at a center with neurovascular imaging capability in-house — rather than a general neurologist.

Reasoning:
- The central diagnostic question — **migraine vs cervicogenic vs mixed** — is exactly the discrimination headache subspecialists do daily and general neurologists do less well. UCNS headache-medicine certification is the credential to look for; there are <1,000 such physicians in the US ([UCNS](https://www.ucns.org/Online/Online/Certification_Home.aspx); [Practical Neurology](https://practicalneurology.com/diseases-diagnoses/headache-pain/we-are-all-headache-specialists/30227/)). The usual advice is that headache specialists are for cases that failed front-line care — four months of high-adherence PT with incomplete response qualifies.
- The VBI/diplopia finding needs **vascular imaging (MRA or CTA head/neck)** — any neurologist can order this, but an academic or institute setting means a vascular/stroke neurologist is one hallway away if the imaging shows something. Bow Hunter's workup (dynamic angiography), in the unlikely event it's needed, is an academic-center procedure.
- A headache specialist can also run the **diagnostic block pathway** (GON block) or refer one door down to interventional pain for medial branch blocks — the test that directly adjudicates the PT's cervicogenic hypothesis.

Practical options near Newport Beach (Aetna PPO, in-network status to verify):
- **Hoag — Pickup Family Neurosciences Institute (Newport Beach)** — headache program in town, with neurovascular services on site. The obvious first call.
- **UCI Health (Orange)** — academic center, headache specialists + vascular neurology.
- Farther but strong: **USC, UCLA, Cedars-Sinai** headache programs ([National Headache Foundation provider finder](https://headaches.org/resources/healthcare-provider-finder/) can filter certified specialists by area).

**What to bring:** the diary export (189 entries), the analysis timeline (especially: the trigger table, the two clean stretches under PT, the stiffness-headache decoupling, golf-without-heat, both ibuprofen failures, ~8 ibuprofen days/month), and the PT's one-line summary + VBI result. This dataset is far better than what neurologists usually get; it will materially change the visit.

## 4. Workup to expect / ask about

1. **MRI brain** — rules out Chiari I, posterior fossa lesions (Hypothesis D).
2. **MRA (or CTA) head + neck** — vertebral artery anatomy: dominance, hypoplasia, dissection, compression (Hypothesis C). The direct follow-up to the VBI finding. If static imaging is normal but positional symptoms persist and are reproducible, ask whether **dynamic (rotational) vascular imaging** is warranted.
3. **MRI cervical spine** — disc/facet/cord check. Expect it to be unremarkable; standard MRI is *not* sensitive for cervicogenic headache ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3253147/)) — a normal result does not refute the PT.
4. **Flexion-extension radiographs ± rotational CT** — only if the instability question is pursued seriously; plus a bedside Beighton hypermobility score.
5. **Diagnostic greater occipital nerve block** — the adjudicator between Hypotheses A and B. Response → cervicogenic component confirmed and treatable (blocks/RFA); non-response → migraine pathway.
6. **Therapeutic-diagnostic migraine trial** — a triptan for the next full attack; if attacks are frequent enough, discussion of a preventive. Dramatic triptan response is strong evidence for migraine.

## 5. If it IS a migraine-type central generator: the treatment map

Assuming Hypothesis A is confirmed, this is what the toolbox looks like, ordered by likely relevance to this specific case. (Evidence levels per the treatment section of `headache_research.md`, AHS 2024 consensus.)

### 5a. Acute treatment — upgrade from ibuprofen

- **Triptans** (sumatriptan, rizatriptan) — first-line for moderate-severe attacks; would have been the answer for the two ibuprofen failures (May 5, Jul 16). **Caveat that matters here:** triptans are vasoconstrictors and are contraindicated with cerebrovascular disease — so the vascular question opened by the VBI test should be imaged *before* starting them. Another reason the MRA comes first in the sequence.
- **Gepants** (ubrogepant, rimegepant) — CGRP receptor antagonists. Three properties fit this case specifically: **no vasoconstriction** (usable even while the vascular question is open), **no observed MOH risk** (relevant with ibuprofen at ~8 days/month and climbing), and **proven prodrome use** — the PRODROME trial (ubrogepant 100 mg taken during premonitory symptoms) showed treating in the prodrome can prevent the headache from developing at all.

### 5b. The prodrome-abort strategy — formalize what already works

The 4/4 success of preventive ibuprofen (Jul 13, Aug 10, Aug 11, Aug 13) is the diary independently discovering prodrome treatment. Walter can reliably feel attacks coming — that's a clinically valuable skill. The upgrade is swapping the prodrome ibuprofen for a gepant: same strategy, better evidence, no MOH exposure. This is a concrete ask for the neurologist.

### 5c. Preventive (daily/monthly) therapy — borderline indicated

Guidelines suggest offering prevention at ≥4 headache days/month with disability or ≥6/month. The diary runs ~2–8/month depending on trigger load — borderline, and counting the near-misses the underlying attack rate is higher. Options if pursued:

- **CGRP monoclonal antibodies** (erenumab, galcanezumab, etc., monthly injection) — now first-line per AHS 2024; ~46% responder rate at 3 months rising to ~71% at 24; well tolerated. Aetna will likely require step therapy (failing 1–2 older preventives first) — worth asking the neurologist to document the case.
- **Atogepant** (daily oral gepant) — preventive without injections.
- **Classic generics** (propranolol, topiramate, amitriptyline) — cheaper, Level A, more side effects; often the mandatory first step for insurance anyway.
- Given the moderate frequency, a reasonable path is: fix acute treatment + prodrome strategy first, hold prevention in reserve unless frequency stays ≥6/month through the fall.

### 5d. Non-drug measures, re-read through the migraine lens

The diary makes these specific rather than generic:

1. **Meal regularity** — newly identified trigger (Jul 16 lunch at 14:00, Jul 27 "åt för lite" before golf). Directly actionable: eat before golf, carry food during. Zero cost, plausibly removes a chunk of the "golf headaches."
2. **Caffeine timing on weekends** — 3–4 cups/day with first cup early on workdays. Sleeping in on weekends delays the first dose; caffeine withdrawal is a classic contributor to the "klassisk helghuvudvärk" let-down pattern. Testable micro-intervention: keep first-coffee time constant seven days a week.
3. **Sleep regularity, not just quantity** — the Jul 16 attack after 9h of catch-up sleep fits the migraine rule that *change* is the trigger. Aim for consistent timing; avoid big weekend catch-up swings (ties into #2).
4. **Trigger stacking budget** — the data consistently shows single triggers are survivable and stacked ones aren't (Jun 14: flight + heat + alcohol; May 3: golf + sun + beer). Treat triggers as a budget to spend, not items to eliminate.
5. **Keep the PT program** — the cervical contribution is real and the neck is a migraine trigger input like any other; the spring gains raised the threshold. This is complementary, not competing.
6. **Supplement stack** (Level B, cheap, low-risk): magnesium 400–600 mg + riboflavin 400 mg + CoQ10 150 mg daily — a combination RCT showed reduced pain intensity and headache impact. 2–3 month trial to judge.
7. **Aerobic exercise** — Level A preventive (as effective as topiramate in one RCT); already in place, keep it. **Biofeedback/CBT/mindfulness** — Level A/B for the stress and let-down pathway specifically.

### 5e. Neuromodulation (later-line, if needed)

FDA-cleared non-drug devices exist (Cefaly trigeminal stimulation, Nerivio REN, gammaCore nVNS, sTMS) with Level B evidence — realistic options if medication is undesirable or insufficient, not first reaches.

### 5f. Sequencing

1. MRA/imaging → clears the vascular question **and** unlocks triptans.
2. Triptan trial on next full attack (diagnostic + therapeutic) or gepant if starting before vascular clearance.
3. Gepant for prodrome use → replaces the growing preventive-ibuprofen habit.
4. Meals/caffeine/sleep-regularity micro-interventions immediately — free, and the diary can measure them.
5. Preventive discussion (CGRP mAb or atogepant) if frequency holds ≥6 days/month after the above.
6. Supplements as a background trial anytime.

## 6. Honest overall read

The most likely single answer, on current evidence, is **not** one cause but the mixed picture the data has pointed at since April: a **migraine-type central generator** (explaining the trigger profile, let-down pattern, oversleeping/fasting sensitivity, decoupled neck stiffness, and incomplete PT response) sitting on top of a **real cervical contribution** (explaining the L≫R findings, occipital radiation, rotation/golf trigger, and the genuine PT gains). The VBI/diplopia finding is most likely a false positive of a weak test — but it is exactly the kind of finding one images once, properly, and then stops worrying about. The neurologist visit should aim to (a) clear the vascular question with MRA, (b) adjudicate migraine-vs-cervicogenic with a triptan trial and/or GON block, and (c) open the migraine-preventive toolbox if (b) points that way.
